### [Cool URIs Don&\#x27;t Change \(1998\)](https://www.w3.org/Provider/Style/URI) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A foundational 1998 W3C article, &quot;Cool URIs Don&\#x27;t Change,&quot; emphasizes the critical importance of stable Uniform Resource Identifiers \(URIs\) for ensuring the long-term longevity and reliability of web resources. This principle advocates for designing URIs that remain constant over time, preventing broken links and preserving the integrity of information on the web. Despite being over two decades old, its core message remains highly relevant for modern web development, information architecture, and digital preservation efforts.

hackernews · Klaster\_1 · Aug 9, 14:32 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49231809)

**「Background」** The 1998 W3C article &quot;Cool URIs Don&\#x27;t Change&quot; by Tim Berners-Lee advocates for the persistence and stability of Uniform Resource Identifiers \(URIs\) on the web. It argues that URIs should be designed to remain unchanged over decades, attributing URI changes to human decisions rather than technical necessity. The article provides practical advice, such as omitting volatile details like dates, authors, file extensions, or software specifics from URLs, to ensure their long-term reliability and prevent broken links.

**「Impact」** Adherence to the &quot;Cool URIs Don&\#x27;t Change&quot; principle directly contributes to the enduring accessibility and trustworthiness of web content, preventing link rot and maintaining the integrity of digital references over decades.

**「Community Discussion」** Community members widely regard the article as a classic whose credibility has only grown with time, noting its own URI has remained stable for 28 years. However, discussions also highlight contemporary failures, such as broken Microsoft support links and a 1998 NSF.gov URL now returning a 404, alongside modern mitigation strategies like 301/302 redirects and CMS-integrated slug renames, though these don&\#x27;t fully prevent issues from neglect or site removals.
